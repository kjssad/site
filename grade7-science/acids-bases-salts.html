Main Idea

Acids and bases have distinguishable properties.

Essential Question

How should acids and bases be used?

Explain · Ideational Connection

Think about your favorite foods and drinks.

  • Do any of your favorite foods or drinks taste sour?
  • Do any taste bitter?
  • Are any of them slimy?

Think about how different tastes and textures might relate to acids, bases, and salts.

Explain

Acids and bases are opposite types of chemicals found in many everyday substances.

Lemons are sour because they contain citric acid.

Coffee is bitter because it contains certain bases.

Salts form in neutralization reactions. They matter in everyday life, such as cooking and food preservation.

Discuss: how can understanding these chemical properties help us in cooking and making food taste better?

Explain · Lesson 3-3

Acids and bases: properties

Acids

  • Taste sour
  • React with certain metals such as magnesium (Mg), zinc (Zn), and tin (Sn), liberating hydrogen gas

Bases

  • Taste bitter
  • Slippery (as observed in soaps)
  • React with grease and oils
  • Turn red litmus paper blue

Both acids and bases conduct electricity in aqueous solutions.

Explain · Litmus paper

Litmus paper is a strip of paper treated with a specific indicator.

It changes color depending on the acidity or basicity of the solution it is soaked in. There are two types: blue and red.

Dip blue litmus paper into an acidic solution: it turns red.

Dip red litmus paper into a basic solution: it turns blue.

Explain · Salts

Salts

  • Salts result from the neutralization reaction of an acid and a base.
  • Neutralization is a chemical reaction in which an acid and a base react with each other to form salt and water.
  • Salts will not change the color of litmus paper, as they are generally neutral.
  • If dissolved in water, they can potentially affect the color change of the litmus paper, depending on the nature of the salt.

Elicit · RWAPS, page 62

Aspirin and its co-crystal

A volunteer reads the article aloud.

  • Aspirin is used to prevent blood clots that may lead to life-threatening heart attacks and strokes.
  • Its active ingredient, acetylsalicylic acid (ASA), is poorly soluble in water.
  • Time is of the essence in a heart attack or stroke, so higher solubility in water would make it take effect more quickly.
  • Researchers at Ruhr University Bochum, Germany combined ASA with a crystalline substance (an inactive ingredient) as a co-crystal. When the co-crystal contacts water, the bond with ASA dissolves, releasing ASA, followed by degradation of the co-crystal.
